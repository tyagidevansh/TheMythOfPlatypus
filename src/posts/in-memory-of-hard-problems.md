I love computer science - or at least I used to, for a few years. Maybe I still love it, I can't be sure really. As a child there were several things I dreamt of being, but the earliest dream I remember having is to be an "inventor". I'd scribble drawings of Iron Man's suits and superbikes, pretending they were actual blueprints. Pretending that I was inventing the future. Later, I happened to watch "Cosmos" on National Geographic and my dream switched to be an astrophysicist. It must've sounded ridiculous hearing a 9 year old claim his dream to be to be an "astrophysicist". Later still, I decided that being an astrophysicist was too difficult for me and I was going to be no good at it, and I bounced around daydreaming about a variety of engineering disciplines until I landed on Comp Sci, mostly out of a sense of defeat.

I am not a remarkable person, nothing rubbed this more in my face than the time I spent preparing for JEE. A "human tier list" of sorts became entrenched within me over the course of the last couple of years of highschool. There were people who just happened to be really smart, and I wasn't one of them. Now granted, I'm not stupid - I'm just not *remarkable*. It is against this background that computer science found me. 

Initially, I would sit down to practise coding for no other reason than to find a job (eventually). I mean, that's why I picked CS right? I'd written the littlest Python in Grade 12, as a part of school curriculum. The first time I tried to write python - in an online interpreter as I owned a chromebook - I couldn't even print user input for a good while. It was frustratring. Right as college was about to start, I finally had a non-potato laptop in my posession and one of the first things I did on it was to install Python. A simple google search came next - "How to make desktop apps in python".

I remember the first project I ever made, it's still on my GitHub. A tkinter GUI to display exchange rates for a bunch of major currencies. The exchange rates weren't fetched from an API, they were read from a CSV. A laughably simple little app, it's embarassing in hindsight to think how proud EIGHTEEN year old Devansh was about it. This sure was a shocking thing to discover at 18, but man, bringing something into existence which simply did not exist before was FUN. Right afterwards I made Pong in Python, and then I kind of stopped for a while.

Programming was hard, it required novel thinking. I really, really loved physics in school, to the point I seriously considered doing a bachelor's in it, but even then I'd only ever sit down to solve problems when I had an exam to prepare for. Working hard unless there is something to work towards isn't something that used to come to me back then. The months kept passing and I wasn't very regular in writing code, until the summer arrived.

ChatGPT had well over 100 million users by then, and that scared me. GPT-3 couldn't write code, but neither could I. Could I really improve faster than an AI? I was incredibly sad back then. It all came to a head on one cool night in March 2023. I was filled with doubts about what I wanted to do. Sure, writing code had been a little fun, but I'd loved so many things before. I certainly had felt a lot more joy studying history, and that wasn't going to be my career. Could CS really be my career? Could I find happiness in thinking hard for a living? Someone like me? Could I outpace AI advancement? Would the job even exist by the time I graduated? Could I give 40 years to it? 

Yes, yes I could. No matter what I was going to give it my all. The time was going to pass anyway right? I was going to become the best programmer I could, no matter what. Even if the AIs kept improving, I would keep up. If I couldn't find a job, I would do it for fun. But I WOULD become a great software engineer. I was going to be in control. That was the start of my multi-year grind, which ended up being a success all things considered.

The very next day I started work on a discord bot which could be used to keep track of focused time. The forest app had become unusable by then so a discord bot seemed the easiest possible replacement to me. That was technically my first CRUD app. Real ones know this project idea stuck with me and became Ally - the first multi-month long project I undertook a year later. AI was far more advanced then, but Ally was still largely handwritten. With the goal of making Ally in mind I spent the better part of a year learning web development. God, I was learning PHP in big 24. React took me so long to learn, but the euphoric feeling of completing the Tic Tac Toe AI using minimax is something I will never forget. My life was changing, I was spending more time being happy, and it was all due to programming.

As the summer started to fade I started doing Hackerrank. That was also the time I started running. Running for the first time, making great progress in the process, while mulling over a programming problem was special. Even in the moment I knew how special it all was. I remember explicitly thinking "I'm going to miss this time so bad". And indeed I do. 

Then came the third semester of college. On the first day of semester we took a class about logic gates. That day, fireship uploaded a video about different kinds of computer chips - GPUs vs TPUs vs CPUs - nothing extraordinary, but the video contained a shoutout to a website called "cpu.land". That was the first technical blog I ever read and my goodness, I was SO EXCITED reading it. It also felt so cool, my heart was racing all the way through as I read it. I wanted to learn more. Till then, every project I'd started was with the explicit goal of upskilling towards a job. Not anymore. I started Nand2Tetris not because I thought it'd help me get a job, I just really, really wanted to. I was no longer having fun studying CS because I knew I was progressing towards employment, the actvity was enjoyable on its own. 

The train kept picking up speed. I loved bingeing The Cherno, to this day C++ holds a fondness in my heart which I cannot explain. I find it so incredibly pretty, the way you'd find a flower pretty. Theory of computation introduced me to the history of computer science and the legendary programmers of decades past became my heros. I learnt Next.js and got my first internship, working in a real workplace was so exciting all over again. Then I started working on Pogberry. I made a few games and my ally graphs just kept going up and up. Even studying for exams was so fun. Surely this euphoria couldn't end.

And then came Opus 4.6.

I'm not anti-AI. I'm aware this entire article is the modern equivalent of a weaver whining about being forced to go from weaving cloth sitting in a countryside shed in rural Yorkshire to having to work in a dark factory in Manchester. I am that weaver, and perhaps I am the sacrifice necessary to ensure humanity keeps progressing. Perhaps in another year or two I'll get used to all of this and won't even miss coding by hand anymore. Afterall, I've had barely a few years of experience of programming by hand. There are people who had honed their skills over decades and just... don't need to use it anymore. What about them?

I was working at Travel Lykke when Opus 4.6 came out. I was already using AI in my workflow plenty by mid 2025, but it still felt balanced, kind of. The AI was the copilot until then, 4.6 truly felt like a paradigm shift to me. That was the first time when I provided an AI model the screenshot of a figma design and it just produced it. It "one-shot" it. I was pretty good at writing Tailwind, you know, and overnight I just didn't need to anymore. Things have gotten so bad even thinking about giving a model the screenshot of a figma design feels archaic now. My Claude has the org's figma sheets saved to its memory and it can pull the frames out whenever it needs them. I'm the copilot now. 

I fought this feeling for a while by just keeping busy through a secret trick known as (ethical) moonlighting. Working 12-14 hour days sure gave me no time to bemoan how my newly developed passion was being taken away. But overworking is hardly sustainable, and it had its own issues. I've been trying to keep busy by working on Pogberry, and doing leetcode ocassionaly, and writing blogs, and learning how to play the piano and many other things but it's just not the same. Nothing changes the fact that my day job is to be an agent orchestrator. 

These feelings had been welling up for quite some time but they come out today due to the latest episode of the The Standup featuring The Legendary Game Programmer Casey Muratori. Everyone on there is a talented programmer, much better than I ever could've become perhaps. On this episode in question these people were discussing their experiences with StackOverflow and it truly filled me with deep pain. Programming was about solving hard problems. It was about sitting in front of a keyboard and just figuring stuff out. You had to work for everything. Every UI element was something you quite literally made with your own hands. Every bug deserved respect. It was a noble occupation. It made you better. 

Does any of that hold true today? 2026 is the first year, perhaps ever, where I can quite confidently say that my intellect has not improved. I'm not a better engineer today than I was a year ago. That's painful to say. And what's even more painful is that I'm not certain if it matters. What really separates a good engineer from a bad one today? What woud separate a good engineer from a bad one 5 years from now? I agree it is still important to know how to code and knowledge is never wasted once aquired, but really what is it for? No one disputes agents' ability to write better code than humans anymore, and surely they are only going to get better from here. I was convinced CS could be my life, but this is not the CS I signed up for. 

Software engineering is not going away, I'm not scared to lose my job (yet), but just because the title stays the same does not mean the job stays the same. "But the code was never the hard part" I DONT CARE! IT WAS FOR ME! I don't want to run a software factory, I want to be on the factory floor getting my hands dirty. 

There are no hard problems anymore. Everthing can be done when you want it. This is a statement for my life in general, and I think for plenty peoeple's lives, we simple do not have to struggle for anything anymore. And I hate it! I don't want to become a thoughtless blob no different from the person next to me. I dreamt of becoming better, better than everyone around me. I wanted to be a great engineer, can I even do that anymore? I wanted to think - think about how to structure my code, how to solve that one annoying bug. I'm quite confident my brain hasn't atrophied, all the side projects have kept it alive enough that I'm pretty certain I haven't gotten any worse at programming, but I haven't gotten any better either. This cannot continue.

I can always put external rules to make things harder. I'm aware I can simply not use AI. But committing to a hard choice once per sometimes, as a hobby, is hardly the same thing? Will anyone ever be considered cracked at programming again? Do I even want to stay in this profession? What's going to become of it? 

The quote at the top of this article is an excerpt from a blog written by a Deepseek engineer. I can only attempt to empathise with them. They are affected by this outsourcing of intelligence to machine far more than me, yet they are helping accelerate the very thing they hate. 

But, I'm not giving up on CS. Not yet. I'm anxious about the future and regretful about the past I did not get to experience, but maybe vibecoding will stockholm syndrome me into liking it. Perhaps I'll be tokenmaxxing next year with 5 Claude subscriptions. Or perhaps GPT-7 will take down the internet and my kids will get to mine spice on Arrakis.
