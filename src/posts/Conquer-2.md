The silence carried on to the next day.

Corin awoke right as the first of the sun’s rays started dancing on the waters surrounding the eastern docks. For a few moments he lay still beneath his blankets, blinking at the wooden beams overhead. Something had roused him, though he could not say what. The room was filled with pale morning light, he threw aside his blanket and jumped out of bed.

The wooden floor was cold beneath his feet as he hurried to the window, almost tripping on a small wooden sword that lay on the floor along the way. The window faced east, like every window in the house. Beyond it, the village roofs glowed softly beneath the rising sun, their iron chains hanging motionless over dark slate tiles. Corin pushed the shutters open and he felt the soft breeze caress his face. The air drifted in gently, carrying the smell of salt, sheep and fresh bread from somewhere down the street. He grinned.

From downstairs came the unmistakable crack of a wooden spoon striking the rim of a pot. 

“Corin!” his mother called. “How many times have I told you not to stomp? You’re going to knock down this house before the wind does!”

“I'm not stomping! And there’s no wind anymore either, this house will stand for centuries!”

He bounded down the stairs, taking the last three in a single jump.

The kitchen was already warm and a loaf of bread rested on the table beside a pot of berry jam. His mother stood with her sleeves rolled past her elbows, stirring a pot that smelled faintly of herbs. She looked over her shoulder just in time to catch him stealing a slice of bread and slapped his hand away with the wooden spoon- not hard enough to hurt, only enough to make him laugh. “Wash first, boy, at least clean your hands and rinse your mouth.”

Corin ran to the back of the house and reappeared in under a minute. Lena, his mother, knew that he certainly hadn’t cleaned himself well but handed him the loaf anyway.

“Thank you, Lady Mother.”

“Oh, listen to him.” She rolled her eyes toward the ceiling. “The noble knight has awoken again; I hadn’t seen him in a few days.”

"I've always been one."

“Knights need to be taller than their swords you know.”

Her laugh filled the room before he could think of a reply. She had one of those laughs that refused to stay indoors. It bounced off walls, escaped through open windows and somehow convinced neighbours they ought to laugh too. Corin had been told many times that his father had laughed exactly the same way.

His sister, Mira, entered carrying an armful of folded washing. She stopped in the doorway. Corin knew that look.

"You've opened the window."

"So?"

"So, close it."

"Why?"

"Because you'll let the flies in."

"There aren't any flies."

"There will be."

"There aren't."

"There will."

Corin bit into the bread. He wasn’t going to carry out that order so easily, the dance had to be carried on for longer. There indeed weren’t any flies. Corin thought about how often he argued with his sister, and smiled to himself. He loved arguing with her. He looked up from the bowl of soup his mother had just placed in front of him, expecting his sister’s glare piercing through him. Surprisingly, she wasn’t looking at him. She stared blankly out of the window. The basket of laundry waited in her hands. It could be hung out to dry outside that day; it wasn’t everyday the people of Kubrea got to dry their clothes out in the sun. 

“So” Lena set the spoon aside. “Still nothing.”

Mira nodded. “I kept waiting for it to return during the night.”

“So did I.”

His mother resumed slicing bread and placed another slice onto his plate before ruffling his hair as she passed. Corin looked back toward the window. The chains hanging from the neighbouring roofs remained perfectly still. He had never realised how much noise they usually made. 

As soon as he was done eating enough bread to constitute a regular kid’s weekly intake, Corin pushed his chair away from the table and heard Mira call to him from outside the house. “Don't go near the western cliffs today.” Lena turned to face Corin as well, as to reiterate the point Mira had just made.

Corin looked up innocently at his mother. “I wasn't planning to.” 

She gave him a long look. “Right.” She reached across the table and flicked a crumb from Corin's forehead. She pointed toward the door. “Okay skedaddle then, and tell Garrick I'll need two good cod before sunset.”

Corin blinked. "You knew I was going to the harbour?”

She laughed and kissed the top of his head as he hurried past. "Be home before dark."

He almost ran into Mira on his way out. She walked over to Lena and crossed her arms. “Garrick won’t stay at the harbour. And you know full well Cor won’t either. He’s going to climb the cliffs. What if he falls mother? Worse, what if Garrick decides to sail round west? I’d be surprised if he hasn’t already visited the Dark Sea for the morning’s catch”.

“Cor will be fine; he’s Alaric’s son after all.”

Corin stepped out onto the street, squinting as the morning sun greeted him. Most of Kubrea was awake. Merchants were arranging baskets outside their shops and somewhere nearby a smith had already begun hammering away at a piece of iron. 

Corin wandered down the street at an easy pace, returning every greeting he received with far more enthusiasm than was necessary.

“Morning, Captain!”

The voice belonged to old Fenn, the village baker, who stood outside his shop brushing flour from his apron. The heavy oak door behind him stood wide open, allowing the warm scent of fresh bread to spill into the street. Rarely were the bakery doors left so wide open, for the wind would generally blow far too much dust and salt into the shop. Corin was happy to smell the bread, it lifted his spirits even higher. "Morning!" he replied with a grin.

The market square was busier than he had expected. Women carried baskets of vegetables between the stalls, fishermen mended torn nets while discussing the previous day's catch, and children darted happily through the crowd. Every few minutes someone would stop in the middle of a conversation and glance westward to nowhere in particular. Corin followed one such gaze himself, though the rows of houses prevented him from seeing anything beyond. He shrugged. The sea would still be there when he reached the harbour.

As he turned into another street, he noticed lines of freshly washed clothes hanging between several houses. Sheets and tunics swayed gently in the faint breeze, catching the morning sunlight in a way Corin had never seen before. Mrs. Elia was fastening another blanket to the line when she noticed him.

“Good morning, Cor.”

“Morning! You're drying them outside.”

She smiled, though her eyes drifted west as she spoke. “Perhaps it has finally grown tired.”

Corin continued on and the streets grew quieter as he approached the eastern side of the village. Here there were fewer market stalls and more fishermen's cottages, small groups of men stood outside them talking amongst themselves in low voices. Corin caught only fragments as he hurried past.

“...further than I've ever gone...”

“...the swell's changed...”

“...there was nothing. Just sea...”

“...it isn't right...”

He slowed for a moment, hoping to hear the rest, but the men noticed him almost immediately.

“Off to find Garrick, are you?” one of them called.

“I told Mother I'd fetch her two cod.” Corin nodded.

A few of the fishermen exchanged amused glances. “If he's fished at all,” one of them muttered.

Corin frowned, but before he could ask what that meant, the conversation had already drifted elsewhere.

The harbour revealed itself at the end of the street, its long wooden piers stretching out into the calm waters of the bay. It was summertime, and the bay shone bright blue. Once winter rolled through, the blue would give way to a terrible grey which rivalled the Dark Sea. Corin didn’t think the absence of the storm was going to change that. 

Ordinarily, the harbour was the busiest place on the island. Boats came and went throughout the day, sailors shouted to one another over the wind, and gulls circled endlessly above the day's catch. It was a place of movement, of noise, of work. That morning, it seemed content merely to watch.

Many of the fishing boats still rested where they had been tied the previous evening, rocking gently against the docks. Their crews stood beside them rather than aboard them, speaking quietly or not at all. Some leaned upon the railings with folded arms, taking in all of the sun’s warmth lest it disappear again.

Corin recognised many of the faces, but only one was where he had expected him to be. At the very end of the longest pier stood Garrick. The old fisherman had his hands clasped behind his back, his broad shoulders silhouetted against the sea beyond. He had not yet noticed Corin's arrival. Corin smiled to himself. There were few places on Kubrea where one could expect to find Garrick at any hour of the day that he was not out at sea, but the end of the longest pier had always been one of them. Today, however, something about the old man's stillness gave him pause. Garrick was not waiting for the waves, nor was he admiring the weather like the old men outside the tavern. He seemed deep in thought.

Corin stepped onto the pier, the old timber creaked beneath his feet. The harbour had always smelled the same - salt, fish, damp rope and tar. Even on mornings when the catch was poor, or the weather too rough for sailing, that smell never changed. It was one of the few things about Kubrea that Corin imagined never could.

Garrick remained facing the sea but must have heard Corin approaching. The old fisherman had ears that defied all expectation, he seemed to hear everything that happened upon the water. More than once he had identified a returning boat simply by the rhythm of its oars striking the waves long before anyone else could make out its sails. Yet he made no sign that he had noticed the boy.

Corin came to a stop beside him and rested his elbows upon the weathered railing.  The bay was calm. Gentle ripples rolled beneath the pier, rocking the boats so lightly that several of them scarcely seemed to move at all.

"Mother knew I'd find you here."

"I imagine she did."

"She wants two cod before sunset."

"I suppose I'd better catch two cod before sunset, then."

Corin turned around and leaned a little farther over the railing, trying to spot a fish below the surface. Schools of tiny silver shapes drifted lazily between the pilings. You rarely ever saw them so close to the coast. The seas to the east of Kubrea were the best fishing grounds in all of the empire, but the fishermen had to venture out into the blue for the catch. 

"They're acting strange."

"So is everything else."

The old fisherman looked much as he always had. His beard had gone almost completely white years ago, though his back remained as straight as the mast of any fishing boat in the harbour. His face was deeply lined from decades spent upon the sea, and the skin of his hands had long ago become as rough as old rope. The roughness was all external, though, the island children all liked him. Most adults did too.

He had no family of his own anymore, though nobody seemed able to remember exactly when that had become the case. Somehow Garrick had gradually become everybody's uncle and nobody's relation. Corin had always liked listening to his stories. Some were almost certainly true. Others became less believable every time he told them.

"I’m thinking about sailing west." Garrick broke, "You’re the first person I’m saying this to so keep quiet, but I think I’m going to drain my little war chest and sail as far west as I can go."

For another minute they stood together without speaking. The silence between them was an easy one, born from familiarity rather than awkwardness. Corin’s heart picked pace, sailing out west seemed incredible to him. “Who knows when the storm may return,” he thought, “we must head out now!”. Another thought started to take shape in his little head, something to with the ship being torn apart if the storm were to return while they were out at sea, but another greater thought took its place further up the line, “What if there’s more land out there?”

Before he could ask another question, the sound of hurried footsteps echoed along the length of the pier. One of the harbour boys was running towards them as fast as his legs would carry him, waving both arms above his head.

"Garrick!" he shouted long before he arrived. "They're ringing the assembly bell!"
