No, it isn't*.

There is something that's strange about my relationship with my country - I don't think of myself as particularly patriotic, but I do find the idea of leaving India uncomfortable. If I was to get a job offer to work in another country at some cushy total comp, I would take it without second thought, but at the same time I've never really *wanted* to leave, I've never *tried* to leave. This seems like a nothingburger dilemma, I know, but it does leave me wondering *why* I wouldn't want to leave. Is it just because this is home? I don't think so. I fantasize about leaving Delhi all the time, and I LOVE Delhi. Going to a new place does sound pleasant, and that's certainly not what's holding me back. Why do I feel connected to the idea of India? What even IS the idea of India? Heck, why does a Tamil person feel any connection to India? How does a nation with multiple ethnicities even survive while being relatively free but at the same time shockingly poor? Why is India magentic?

Okay, time to back up. I think I've posed enough questions but I'd like to pose another because I think the title of the article deserves repeating - Is India a social construct? Do we feel attachment to a concept that didn't exist till a private company from a rainy island somewhere decided to take over these lands? That's clearly inadequate. There was some idea of "India" that existed before the British. There were civilizational, religious, commercial and intellectual connections stretching across huge parts of the subcontinent. But ugh, what about the North-East? Nagaland was not Indian by any sense of the word before the British arrived, so what even is India? I like Hindi, even as a complete atheist I find some concepts of Hinduism cool, but none of these things are India, are they? 

Is India just a chunk of Asia that just so happened to form a singular country? No, that's underselling it again. The British weren't trying to create an independent, stable state. Indians took the political framework that the brits created and forged a state out of it. Naturally modern India's borders are obviously shaped by the British. Nepal wasn't a part of British India, Ceylon wasn't, Burma was but it ended up getting separated in 1937. 1937! That's not even 90 years ago, and barely anyone ever mentions it as "rightfully Indian" or "lost land". Large parts of the Northeast were just as "un-indian" as Burma, yet they're an integral part of India. All of this is starting to seem a little too imperialist.

This is what I wish to dive into in this blog. I'm not interested in the Hindi belt or Bengal. It's plenty obvious how Punjab to Bengal and Himachal to Maharashtra is all one civilizational fabric. The Sanskritic link is undeniable and awesome (I recently found out about how just old Puri's Rath Yatra is, I would recommend following up on it after this, pre-modern Hinduism is seriously cool). But what about the Dravidian lands? Sure the same East India Company ruled over them, but why would a Tamil choose to fight for a unified country with someone who speaks Khari Boli 2 thousand kilometers away? And even if pooling together resources to fight a bigger enemy in the big bad British was the obvious choice then, it certainly does not explain why all these places chose to stay once the British left. Pakistan didn't stay, they left at the cost of hundreds of thousands of human lives yet princely states chose to join. 

I think I'm going to carry on the Tamil example forward as I try to rationalise my thoughts. I swear I'm not imagining Tamilians to be some exotic people group, it just makes sense to me to keep the Tamils in mind simply because so much of what I grew up learning simply does not apply there. Tamil civilization is ancient. Tamil is one of the world's oldest continuously used literary languages. The Cholas, Pandyas, Cheras and Pallavas spread their influence across much of South-East Asia, perhaps it is because of them we get to be the only country with an ocean named after ourselves (Bet trump is jealous). But well, I can only say "perhaps" in this situation because I truly don't know much about the Cholas or the Pandavas. 

For a northerner like me, all of India's history revolves around Delhi. The Delhi Sultanate, the Mughals, the Brits. Even today, much of modern politics is dictated by Hindutva and Pakistan - neither of which are issues that Tamil Nadu is particularly concerned with. They don't see terror attacks and last I checked the Tamils aren't worried about saving Hinduism from the boogeyman. Going further back into history the Indus Valley Civilization was in the north, the Vedas were written somewhere in the north (likely outside India, in fact), a lot of the Hindu tradition that I'm aware of revolves around things in the North. The Ganges, all the sacred mountains, Avadh and Dwarka are all in the north. H

Yet, a Tamil person today can be deeply Tamil and deeply Indian without necessarily seeing any contradiction between the two. I used to think of Indian nationalism as something that naturally grew out of a common culture. But the more I read about Tamil politics, the less convincing that seems. I don't know why but I seemed to have the naive idea (rather, I never gave it any thought) that the very core of Indian civilization is NOT solely sanskrit. I mean, can you blame me? The hindi word for civilization is *sanskriti*!  

The Dravidian movement, especially in the twentieth century, was explicitly opposed to what it saw as North Indian, Brahminical and Sanskritic cultural dominance. It wasn't just asking for better roads or more state government powers. There was even the idea of Dravida Nadu, a separate Dravidian state. And this wasn't some tiny fringe thought experiment. The Dravidian movement became one of the defining political forces of Tamil Nadu. Yet look at Tamil Nadu today, it is probably one of the clearest examples of a state where regional identity is extraordinarily strong while Indian identity has not disappeared at all.

So when did Tamils become Indian? Was it 15th August 1947? 

Well, I jest but naturally the British are a part of the equation. The British created an enormous administrative unit called British India. They built railways, a bureaucracy, a legal system, a colonial army, universities, newspapers and political institutions that connected places which had previously been separated by different kingdoms and political systems. The Madras Presidency was part of this system. But obviously that does not explain everything. Tamil Nadu wasn't dragged along with the rest of the country whence Independence arrived, they fought for it. Plenty of Tamil freedom fighters preached the dual mandate of Tamil + Indian nationalism (I wonder how the average Tamil middle-aged man reacted to the fact that the office of the Prime Minister was occupied by someone from Uttar Pradesh or Bihar for almost the entirety of the first 50 years of the country's existence).

Keeping this fact in mind is important because it places Tamil Nadu in a proactive position. They wanted the British gone, and that did not require them to stop being Tamil. In the early nationalist movement, the major political conflict wasn't Tamil versus Hindi, it was Indian versus British. And even Hindi itself was not synonymous with all of northern India. The region that we casually call the "Hindi belt" contains a huge number of linguistic traditions. If we really wish to talk about Hindi homogeniety killing cultures we must first think about Brij Bhasha, Maithili, Avadhi and all the other languages that got swept up. Tamil was always determined to not be one of them, and this clarity during the freedom struggle shaped the very way the Indian Union was organized. You can be Tamil and have a Tamil state, yet still be a part of India. 

But why didn't Tamil nationalism lead to another Pakistan? If a state with a different language can exist then why can't different religions which have already been co-existing continue to exist as they've already been doing? I think it is this exact co-habitation that paradoxically dooms the idea of a truly unified British India. I have no research backing for this, but I think it is much easier for people to accept a legally defined separate area in a country for their kind, rather than expect to live in harmony with someone they view as alien. The fact that different regions are different, rather than one city having all the kinds of people, makes it so that the idea of a unified India becomes much more viable. 

But this does not mean Tamil nationalism disappeared. Tamil Nadu's politics remain unusually regional. The Dravidian parties have dominated the state for decades. Tamil language is politically sacred in a way that is difficult to compare with most Indian states. There is still a deep sensitivity to Hindi imposition and to excessive centralization. And yet none of this necessarily translates into wanting India to disappear.

Maybe "Indian" isn't a cultural identity in the way I thought it was, being Indian does not necessarily mean identifying with some particular North Indian historical narrative. A Tamil doesn't have to look at the Mauryas, Guptas and Mughals and think: "These are my ancestors' empires, therefore I am Indian." They can look at the Cholas and Sangam literature and have a completely different historical centre of gravity, and yet India can still be theirs.

A Bengali can have a very different relationship with Indian history. Calcutta was the capital of British India for much of the colonial period. Bengal was one of the great centres of the independence movement. Bankim wrote Vande Mataram. Tagore wrote Jana Gana Mana. Subhas Chandra Bose came from Bengal. So for a Bengali, the path from regional history to Indian nationalism is relatively intuitive. And yet, it's not like Bengal is just like Delhi or Uttar Pradesh. They too have their own history and culture, and yet they were kind of the first to think of India. Why not Tamil Nadu? Why not any other place?

We ARE a diverse country, maybe the most diverse there is. To my Northern brain Tamil Nadu seems different from my idea of India. I struggle to think how the same issues really affect or shape them, but really is that different for any other state? Even being part of the same empires we in the north have had completely different histories and traditions. Keeping this in mind, I feel comfortable stating that North and South India are indeed the same civilization, and us being a single country was not something the Brits solely accomplished.

But a thorn remains, the Northeast. Assam has the Ahoms, which ruled much of the Brahamaputra valley for centuries, but you can argue about their Hindu traditions and links to the rest of the mainland. Mughals were in contact with and fought the Ahoms, all the points that apply to the Tamils also apply to the Akhomiya. But this does not apply to the states on the periphery. Manipur was an independent kingdom. Tripura had its own monarchy, so did the Nagas. Much of what is Arunachal Pradesh today was never meaningully governed or influenced by Indian empires. If anything, they had far more Burmese influence. 

Which brings me back to Burma, because it really was a part of the British empire. Until 1937, it can be argued Burma was more a part of the British Raj than Hyderabad or Mysore. The British properly conquered it in stages and after the annexation of Upper Burma in 1885, it was administered from Calcutta. Later, they formally spearated Burma from India. Looking at a map and seeing Burma as definetely Southeast Asian isn't some timeless civilizational fact, it is a direct result of British administrative decisions. Burmese nationalism developed around a Burmese language, Theravada Buddhism, Burmese monarchy and Southeast Asian historical identity. Indian migration into colonial Burma also became politically contentious. And its not hard to imagine the same trajectory playing out in Arunachal, though I don't imagine the same things quite applying to the Dravidian states so I'm glad to see my weak conclusions holding up.

We also have to consider Nepal and Sri Lanka. Culturally, Nepal and Sri Lanka are far more like "India" than Arunachal Pradesh or Sikkim. Sri Lanka was ruled by the Cholas for a while, Tamil communities have existed on both sides of the Palk Strait for centuries, Nepal is deeply intertwined with the history of North India and the Himalyas to the point that sometimes people forget they are a sovereign country. Yet they ended up as separate countries *because* the British chose to govern them that way. 

**Cultural connection does not automatically produce the same country** and conversely **being part of the same colonial administration does not automatically produce the same national identity**.

So, is India a colonial construct? There can never be a simple yes or no answer to this. The modern Indian state absolutely owes a huge amount to the British. For better or for worse, our territorial boundaries, bureaucracy, army and legal systems were shaped by colonial rule. India's poor bureacracy is perhaps the largest single reason for our failure to industralize quickly but at the same time it is the thing that has made us into a post-colonial success story. The survival of a country so diverse and poor is a miracle. But saying the British invented India misses something equally important. They did not invent the civlization that lived here, and they did not invent the idea of a country which welcomes all. A country made of Tamils that feel Tamil before they feel Indian and Marathas who feel Marathas who feel more Martatha than they feel Indian yet they all choose to stay together in this union. The Union of India was pushed into existence by our colonial overlords, but we gave it shape. They did not create a place which gives peace and freedom to dozens of ethnicities and they did not create the constitution that guarantees rights to everyone. Sri Lanka could not manage two cultures, Pakistan could not manage two religions, but we could. And we must. And none of this was created by the Brits.
